Emission: Where Biological Color Begins.

A lecture-prep podcast based on Chapter 3, "Emission," of Sönke Johnsen's The Optics of Life.

Welcome. This is a lecture-prep episode on emission, designed for an upper-division course on the ecology and evolution of color. The organizing question is simple: before light can be absorbed, scattered, reflected, or detected, where does it come from?

Johnsen's chapter makes a useful simplification. For biology, there are two overwhelmingly important ways to make light. One is thermal radiation: light generated because matter has a temperature. The other is luminescence: light generated by specific transitions between molecular energy states. Ecologically, these map onto two dominant sources: the sun and bioluminescent organisms.

That distinction gives you a clean lecture architecture. First: thermal emission and the sun. Second: the way atmosphere, time of day, and the moon transform the illumination environment. Third: bioluminescence as controlled, nonthermal light production. And then, if you have time, mechanoluminescence and sonoluminescence as reminders that biology finds surprising ways to exploit physics.

Part One. Thermal Radiation.

The first principle is that everything emits electromagnetic radiation. A mammal, an ice cube, liquid helium: anything above absolute zero emits. Temperature reflects molecular and electronic motion, and accelerating charges produce electromagnetic radiation.

The second principle is that hotter objects emit dramatically more radiation. In energy units, total blackbody emission scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature. That fourth-power relationship is worth emphasizing, because students often imagine temperature and emitted radiation as roughly linear. They are not. Double the absolute temperature and you get sixteen times the emitted power.

The third principle is the deep connection between absorption and emission. At thermal equilibrium, absorptivity and emissivity must be equal, wavelength by wavelength. Otherwise you could construct an object that systematically absorbed more than it emitted, or vice versa, and violate thermodynamic equilibrium.

This gives you a nice conceptual bridge to animal coloration. A surface that is highly reflective at a wavelength is, by definition, a poor absorber at that wavelength, and therefore a poor thermal emitter at that wavelength. But visible appearance does not automatically tell you infrared behavior. Arctic animals can look white to us and still behave almost like black objects in the thermal infrared. Plants are an instructive case. Leaves reflect strongly in the near infrared, just beyond the visible, and this is usually discussed in terms of reducing solar heating. Be careful in class, though: a leaf's own thermal emission sits at much longer wavelengths, around ten micrometers, where leaves are nearly perfect emitters. So high near-infrared reflectance tells you about heat gain from sunlight, not about how efficiently the leaf radiates heat away.

Now introduce the blackbody. A perfect blackbody absorbs all incident radiation, and therefore has the maximum possible emissivity. Its spectrum depends only on temperature. The exact Planck equation is probably less important for an ecology-and-evolution class than three consequences.

Consequence one: as temperature increases, total emission increases enormously.

Consequence two: the wavelength of peak emission shifts toward shorter wavelengths as temperature increases. In loose language, hotter objects become bluer.

Consequence three, and this is an important correction to a common intuition: when an object gets hotter, emission rises at every wavelength. The peak shifts, but the red part does not decline. A hotter horseshoe can turn from red toward orange while simultaneously emitting more red photons than it did before.

For biological intuition, a mammalian body at around three hundred ten kelvin peaks in the thermal infrared, a little under ten micrometers when the spectrum is expressed per unit wavelength. The sun, at roughly fifty-eight hundred kelvin, peaks near five hundred nanometers and floods the visible. This is why visible vision works so well at Earth's surface: our dominant external source is hot enough to fill the visible spectrum with photons.

There is a subtle teaching point here. The location of a spectral peak depends on how the spectrum is represented. A spectrum binned uniformly by wavelength does not have the same peak as one binned uniformly by frequency. So spectral peaks are useful when comparing like with like, but students should not treat "the peak" as an intrinsic property independent of units.

Another useful term is color temperature. The color temperature of a light is the temperature of the blackbody whose spectrum best matches it. For a tungsten filament or the sun, color temperature is closely related to physical temperature. For a blue sky or another nonthermal source, it is not. A blue sky can have a color temperature of ten thousand kelvin or more without the atmosphere literally being that hot.

This matters immediately for biological photography and color measurement: illumination spectra change, cameras white-balance, and uncalibrated photographs can give misleading comparisons.

Part Two. Sunlight, Skylight, and the Ecology of Illumination.

Now shift from physics to ecology.

The sun is approximately a fifty-eight-hundred-kelvin blackbody source. But organisms do not experience the solar spectrum as it is above the atmosphere. The atmosphere selectively absorbs and scatters it. Ozone removes essentially all of the UVC and much of the UVB; water vapor, carbon dioxide, oxygen, and other atmospheric components carve absorption bands into the red and infrared.

A key ecological point is that the sun occupies a tiny angular area of the sky, about half a degree across. Skylight is much dimmer per unit solid angle, but it comes from the entire hemisphere. Consequently, skylight contributes substantially to downwelling irradiance, especially at short wavelengths, because short wavelengths are scattered most strongly.

Under the clear-sky conditions Johnsen discusses, skylight contributes roughly fifteen to twenty-five percent of the irradiance in the blue, twenty-five to fifty percent in the UVA, and from about half to nearly all of the UVB.

This makes a good misconception question for class:

If you sit under a small beach umbrella and remove direct sunlight, have you removed most of your UV exposure?

Not necessarily. You feel cooler because you have blocked much of the direct solar heating, but diffuse skylight can still deliver a substantial UV dose.

Then make time of day part of color ecology. The sun moves about one degree across the sky every four minutes. Solar elevation changes both intensity and spectral composition. For much of the day, illumination is surprisingly stable compared with the extraordinary transition around dawn and dusk.

Once the sun approaches and passes below the horizon, irradiance collapses by orders of magnitude. Johnsen divides twilight into civil, nautical, and astronomical phases, based on how far the sun is below the horizon: zero to six degrees, six to twelve, and twelve to eighteen. But for your course, the biologically exciting part is not the naming convention. It is that dusk is simultaneously a change in intensity and in spectral composition.

Late twilight can become extraordinarily blue and then purplish. Johnsen emphasizes that the mechanism is not simply the ordinary blue-sky story. During twilight, long atmospheric path lengths and ozone absorption, including a broad visible absorption feature called the Chappuis band, centered near six hundred nanometers, reshape the spectrum by removing the yellows and oranges.

The result is a rapidly changing sensory environment.

This is a beautiful place to connect physics to visual ecology. An animal active at dusk is not merely coping with fewer photons. It is coping with a moving target: brightness changes by orders of magnitude while the spectrum also shifts.

Predator and prey may differ in spectral sensitivity, temporal resolution, pupil dynamics, spatial and temporal summation, and color vision. So dusk can create a temporary sensory niche.

A well-known example, from Almut Kelber's group, is the elephant hawkmoth, which can discriminate colors at light levels equivalent to dim starlight.

You can use this to ask students:

If color vision is expensive in terms of photon capture, what ecological benefit could justify maintaining it into twilight or night?

Part Three. Moonlight and Moonless Night.

Moonlight is reflected sunlight, so its spectrum begins with the solar spectrum, but is modified by lunar reflectance and by the atmosphere. The moon's surface is slightly reddish, so moonlight is a little redder than sunlight, even though it looks silvery to our dark-adapted eyes.

The ecologically important point is that nocturnal illumination varies much more than the simple lit fraction of the lunar disk might suggest.

A full moon is disproportionately bright because of the lunar opposition effect: surface roughness, shadow hiding, and coherent backscatter make the lunar surface much brighter near full phase than a simple linear model predicts. A full moon is roughly ten times brighter than a half-lit quarter moon, not twice as bright.

Lunar elevation also matters. Full moons are opposite the sun, and therefore spend the night high in the sky, with seasonal effects on their maximum elevation: full moons ride high in winter and low in summer.

Johnsen argues that the integrated light dose over a winter night can differ enormously between full and crescent moon conditions. That provides a physical basis for why lunar phase can be such a strong biological clock or cue. Many marine organisms time reproduction to lunar cycles, including corals and numerous other aquatic taxa.

Moonless night is not simply zero light.

Johnsen highlights three contributors: integrated starlight, zodiacal light, and airglow.

Integrated starlight includes the combined contribution of enormous numbers of stars too dim to resolve individually.

Zodiacal light is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust in the plane of the solar system.

Airglow is light generated by chemical processes in the upper atmosphere, including a prominent atomic oxygen emission line near five hundred fifty-eight nanometers.

The take-home point for color ecology is that nocturnal illumination has a spectrum, and that spectrum changes.

Across sunset, twilight, moonrise, moonset, and deep night, the environment can move through dramatic changes in both brightness and chromatic composition.

For an animal visual system, there is no single thing called "night light."

Part Four. Light Pollution as an Evolutionary and Ecological Perturbation.

Johnsen's treatment discusses the lamp technologies prevalent when the book was written, before the wholesale shift to LEDs, but the conceptual point is broader: artificial light changes not only how much light exists at night, but its spectrum.

The chapter discusses effects on vertical migration in zooplankton, foraging behavior, attraction of insects and birds to bright sources, and disorientation of hatchling sea turtles.

For your lecture, distinguish two axes of artificial light at night: intensity and spectral composition.

Organisms evolved under natural combinations of starlight, moonlight, twilight, and darkness. Artificial lighting can create combinations of intensity, spectrum, direction, and timing that have no close natural analogue.

A useful discussion prompt is:

When predicting the ecological impact of artificial light, is lux a biologically meaningful unit?

The answer depends on the organism. Lux weights light according to human daytime, or photopic, vision. For a moth, fish, turtle, or zooplankter, a photon-based spectrum combined with the animal's own spectral sensitivity is usually more informative.

Part Five. Bioluminescence: Light Made to Order.

Now contrast the sun with the second major biological source of photons: bioluminescence.

Bioluminescence is enzyme-mediated chemiluminescence.

Rather than heating all the particles in an object and accepting a broad thermal spectrum, organisms use chemical reactions to place energy into specific molecular states. When those states relax, photons are emitted.

Photon energy corresponds to the difference between energy states, so chemistry can generate a much narrower and more useful range of wavelengths.

This is why thermal emission is a terrible way for an animal to make a visible signal. To glow visibly by heat, an organism would need temperatures of many hundreds of degrees, and most of the energy would be wasted outside the useful visual band.

Bioluminescence solves that problem through selectivity.

The canonical chemistry uses a luciferin, a small molecule that is oxidized, and a luciferase, an enzyme that mediates the reaction. Different lineages use different molecular systems, and bioluminescence has evolved independently many times.

Light may be produced by symbiotic bacteria, by specialized animal cells called photocytes, or even released outside the body as luminous secretions.

The evolutionary distribution is striking. Bioluminescence is overwhelmingly common in marine systems, especially the open ocean, much less common on land, and extraordinarily rare in freshwater.

Johnsen notes that explanations for the freshwater rarity remain unsatisfying. That is a ready-made research question for students, because it invites competing ecological, historical, and biochemical hypotheses.

Functionally, the chapter presents a broad set of proposed uses: startle displays, counterillumination, misdirection, luring prey, illuminating prey, and mate attraction or recognition.

Johnsen is careful to distinguish plausible function from well-demonstrated function. That distinction is pedagogically valuable. A structure that looks like an adaptation for something is not by itself evidence that it improves fitness through that function.

Counterillumination is especially good for teaching, because the optics are clean.

In open water, an animal viewed from below is silhouetted against bright downwelling light. A white belly cannot solve the problem, because it can only reflect the relatively weak upwelling illumination, which in clear ocean water is only about one or two percent of the downwelling light.

Many pelagic animals instead generate light from ventral photophores and tune it to match the background. Some squid adjust not only intensity but color.

That is an extraordinary example of sensory ecology driving signal evolution. The animal is effectively performing active camouflage by becoming its own light source.

Part Six. Why Is Bioluminescence Mostly Blue-Green?

Most marine bioluminescence peaks roughly between four hundred fifty and five hundred fifty nanometers, often near four hundred eighty. Red bioluminescence is rare.

The obvious ecological hypothesis is transmission.

Clear ocean water transmits blue light best; coastal water often shifts the optimum toward green. So signals should evolve toward wavelengths that travel farthest, and that receivers are best able to detect.

But Johnsen warns against treating this as the whole explanation.

Terrestrial bioluminescence is also often green or yellow-green, and several red-emitting systems appear to begin with shorter-wavelength light and then convert it through fluorescence or filtering, which is inefficient. That suggests biochemical constraint as well as ecological selection.

This is a strong evolutionary teaching example, because it separates three interacting components:

the physical transmission window of the habitat, the spectral sensitivity of receivers, and the biochemical accessibility of different emission wavelengths.

Observed signal color is a product of all three, not necessarily a simple optimum.

You can turn that into a general framework for the course:

signal production, environmental transmission, receiver sensitivity.

Every color signal lives inside that triangle.

Part Seven. Mechanoluminescence and Sonoluminescence.

The chapter ends with two delightful reminders that emission is broader than thermal radiation and chemistry.

Triboluminescence occurs when materials fracture and charge separation leads to electrical discharge and photon emission. The familiar example is crushing wintergreen candy in the dark: the fracturing sugar crystals discharge in the ultraviolet, and the wintergreen flavoring fluoresces, converting that ultraviolet into visible blue light.

Johnsen then connects the idea to hydrothermal vents, where some vent emissions exceed what thermal blackbody radiation predicts, especially at shorter visible wavelengths.

Whether cracking minerals contribute remains unresolved, and whether vent animals use that light is also uncertain.

This is an excellent example of a hypothesis that is physically plausible but not yet established biologically.

Sonoluminescence is light produced during the violent collapse of bubbles in water.

Snapping shrimp generate cavitation bubbles with their claws; bubble collapse creates a very brief flash, as well as the famous snap.

Mantis shrimp strikes can also cavitate water, although Johnsen notes that associated sonoluminescence had not been documented in the evidence reviewed in the chapter.

These examples make a good closing move: once you understand emission mechanistically, strange natural-history observations become testable physics questions.

Lecture Blueprint.

If you want to turn this into a fifty-minute lecture, I would structure it around one repeated question:

What determines the photons available to an organism?

Spend about ten minutes on thermal emission: everything emits; hotter means much more emission; absorptivity equals emissivity; blackbody spectra; temperature changes both total output and spectral distribution.

Spend ten minutes on the sun and atmosphere: the solar spectrum, skylight, UV, and color temperature.

Spend ten minutes on the daily cycle: sunset, twilight, moonlight, moonless night, and the idea that illumination is a dynamic ecological variable rather than a constant background.

Spend fifteen minutes on bioluminescence: mechanism, evolutionary distribution, function, counterillumination, and the blue-green bias.

Use the last five minutes for synthesis and one or two oddities, such as vent light or snapping-shrimp sonoluminescence.

Here are five questions you could put directly to students during the lecture.

One: Why can a white Arctic mammal still be an excellent thermal emitter?

Two: If the sun is setting and the world is getting darker, why might the spectral problem facing an animal become harder rather than simpler?

Three: Why can't a white belly eliminate the silhouette of a fish viewed from below in open water?

Four: Marine bioluminescence is mostly blue-green. What observations would let us distinguish adaptation to water transmission from biochemical constraint?

Five: If you wanted to quantify artificial light pollution for a nocturnal animal, what would you measure instead of relying on lux alone?

And here is the synthesis I would want students to leave with.

Color does not begin at the surface of an animal. It begins with the source.

The photons available in an environment are determined by emission physics, filtered by atmosphere or water, altered by time of day and celestial geometry, and sometimes manufactured by organisms themselves.

Evolution acts downstream of all of this.

Pigments, structural colors, visual systems, signals, and camouflage can only evolve in relation to the photons that actually exist in the habitat.

So when you teach color, do not start by asking only:

"What color is the organism?"

Start one step earlier:

"What light is there to work with?"

That is the central contribution of emission to an ecology and evolution of color course.
